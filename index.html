1 Angular-momentum operators & commutation relations (角運動量の交換関係, p.360, 365-366)

Angular-momentum vector operator $\hat l=(\hat l_x,\hat l_y,\hat l_z)$, $\hat l^2=\hat l_x^2+\hat l_y^2+\hat l_z^2$.
Starting point — Cartesian (classical) definition, before switching to operators: $\boldsymbol l=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$, componentwise
$l_x=$$yp_z-zp_y$,   $l_y=$$zp_x-xp_z$,   $l_z=$$xp_y-yp_x$
By definition (角運動量の定義)による — promoting position/momentum to operators ($\hat p_x=\dfrac{\hbar}{i}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}$, etc.) turns these into $\hat l_x=\dfrac{\hbar}{i}\left(y\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}-z\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)$ and its cyclic partners, which is the form used below.
Q1 Which commutator is being asked about?
① Two components (e.g. $\hat l_x$ and $\hat l_y$)
Result: $[\hat l_x,\hat l_y]=$$i\hbar \hat l_z$ (cyclic: $[\hat l_y,\hat l_z]=i\hbar\hat l_x$, $[\hat l_z,\hat l_x]=i\hbar\hat l_y$)
By the commutation relation for angular momentum components(角運動量の交換関係)により (p.365) — since this is not 0, the two components cannot be measured simultaneously with precision.
Worked: substituting $\hat l_x=\dfrac{\hbar}{i}\left(y\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}-z\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)$ and $\hat l_y=\dfrac{\hbar}{i}\left(z\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}-x\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)$ directly and expanding, the cross terms cancel and $[\hat l_x,\hat l_y]=i\hbar\hat l_z$ remains.
② The squared magnitude and one component (e.g. $\hat l^2$ and $\hat l_z$)
Result: $[\hat l^2,\hat l_i]=$$0$ (holds for any $i=x,y,z$)
By the same commutation relation for angular momentum(角運動量の交換関係)により (p.366) → $\hat l^2$ and one of $\hat l_x,\hat l_y,\hat l_z$ can be determined simultaneously (by convention, $\hat l_z$ is chosen).
Worked: $[\hat l^2,\hat l_z]=[\hat l_x^2,\hat l_z]+[\hat l_y^2,\hat l_z]=-i\hbar\hat l_x\hat l_y-i\hbar\hat l_y\hat l_x+i\hbar\hat l_y\hat l_x+i\hbar\hat l_x\hat l_y=0$.
[!] Trap: "the magnitude $L$ and $l_z$" CAN be measured simultaneously, but "$l_x$ and $l_y$" absolutely CANNOT — always check which pairing the question means.

2 Space quantization of angular momentum, spin, and magnetic moment (角運動量の空間量子化, p.361-365, 392, 600-603)

Q1 Is it orbital angular momentum $l$ / electron spin $s$ / nuclear spin $I$?
Orbital angular momentum ($l=0,1,2,\dots$, $m_l=0,\pm1,\dots,\pm l$, i.e. $2l+1$ values)
$L=$$\hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)}$,   $l_z=$$m_l\hbar$
By the polar-coordinate representation of angular momentum(角運動量の極座標表示)による (p.361) → $L$ depends only on $l$, not on $m_l$ or $r$.
Worked: for a d orbital ($l=2$): $L=\sqrt{6}\hbar$, $l_z=0,\pm\hbar,\pm2\hbar$ (5 states, $2l+1=5$).
Electron spin angular momentum ($s=1/2$ fixed, $m_s=\pm1/2$)
$S=$$\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\hbar$,   $S_z=$$\pm\dfrac12\hbar$,   $\cos\theta=$$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$ $\Rightarrow\theta=54.7°$ (the magic angle(マジックアングル))
As worked in Example 6-9, space quantization of electron spin(電子スピンの空間量子化) (p.392).
Worked: in solid-state NMR, tilting the sample tube to 54.7° and spinning it rapidly (MAS) averages out chemical-shift anisotropy, giving a high-resolution spectrum.
Nuclear spin angular momentum ($I$ is given per nuclide, $m_I=I,I-1,\dots,-I$)
$I=\hbar\sqrt{I(I+1)}$,   $I_z=m_I\hbar$
As worked in Example 6-10, nuclear-spin angular momentum(核スピンの角運動量) (p.392).
Worked: $^{23}\mathrm{Na}$ has $I=3/2$, so the magnitude is $\dfrac{\sqrt{15}}{2}\hbar$ and the $z$-components are $\pm\dfrac12\hbar,\ \pm\dfrac32\hbar$ (4 values).
Q2 Is the magnetic moment $\mu$, or the Bohr/nuclear magneton, being asked for?
Orbital magnetic moment: $\boldsymbol\mu_L=-\dfrac{\mu_B}{\hbar}\boldsymbol L$,   $\mu_{L,z}=$$m_l\mu_B$
By orbital angular momentum and magnetic moment(軌道角運動量と磁気モーメント)による (p.603), where the Bohr magneton(ボーア磁子) is $\mu_B=\dfrac{e\hbar}{2m_e}=9.247\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}$.
Worked: since the electron carries negative charge, $\boldsymbol L$ and $\boldsymbol\mu_L$ point in opposite directions (negative magnetogyric ratio).
Spin magnetic moment: $\mu_{S,z}=$$m_s\,g_e\,\mu_B$ ($g_e=2.0023\dots\approx2$, an experimental value)
By electron-spin angular momentum and magnetic moment(電子スピンの角運動量と磁気モーメント)による (p.603) → the spin magnetogyric ratio is twice the orbital one.
Worked: the ratio of the Bohr magneton to the nuclear magneton(核磁子) $\mu_N=\dfrac{e\hbar}{2m_p}=5.051\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}$ is $\mu_B/\mu_N=1836$; with $g_H=5.586$, the electron's spin magnetic moment is roughly $g_e\mu_B/(g_H\mu_N)\approx658$ times larger than the proton nuclear-spin magnetic moment.
[!] Trap: orbital and spin can share the same $z$-component magnitude ($m=1/2$), but their $g$-factors differ, so their magnetic-moment magnitudes differ — don't drop $g_e$ into $\mu_{L,z}$'s formula.

3 LS coupling and fine structure (one-electron system) (LS coupling, p.406-408)

Prerequisite — hydrogen-like (Bohr) energy levels (one electron, nuclear charge $Z$)
$E_n=$$-\dfrac{Z^2}{n^2}\times13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$   (equivalently $E_n=-\dfrac{m_ee^4Z^2}{8\varepsilon_0^2h^2n^2}$)
By the hydrogen-like atom energy formula(水素類似原子のエネルギー)による.
Worked: $\mathrm{He^+}$ ($Z=2$) has configuration $1s^1$; since energy scales as $Z^2$, its ionization energy is $2^2=4$ times that of neutral H's first ionization energy (13.6 eV) — i.e. $\mathrm{He^+}$'s ionization energy $\approx54.4$ eV.
Q1 Asked to find the term symbol of a one-electron state given $l$ and $s$?
Total angular-momentum quantum number: $j=$$l+s,\ l+s-1,\ \dots,\ |l-s|$
Term notation: $^{2s+1}L_j$ (multiplicity $2s+1$ as left superscript, $j$ as right subscript)
By the coupling of orbital and spin angular momentum(軌道角運動量とスピン角運動量の合成)による (p.406) — this $j$-range formula is specifically named the Clebsch-Gordan series(クレブシュ・ゴードン級数)により.
Worked: hydrogen $2p^1$ ($l=1,s=1/2$): $j=3/2,1/2$ → terms are $^2P_{3/2},\ ^2P_{1/2}$. For hydrogen, the smaller $j$ lies lower in energy.
Q2 Asked for the number of levels/states (degeneracy)?
Degeneracy of a level $=2j+1$ ($m_j=j,j-1,\dots,-j$)
As worked in Example 6-12, fine structure of the hydrogen atom's M shell(水素原子のM殻の微細構造) (p.407).
Worked: for $n=3$ ($l=0,1,2$) with one electron: the terms are $^2S_{1/2},{}^2P_{3/2},{}^2P_{1/2},{}^2D_{5/2},{}^2D_{3/2}$ (5 terms), and the total number of states is $2+4+2+6+4=18$. Energy levels split as $j=1/2$ (4-fold), $j=3/2$ (8-fold), $j=5/2$ (6-fold), rising in that order.
Individual selection rule (condition for an allowed transition): $\Delta l=$$\pm1$,   $\Delta m_l=$$0,\pm1$
By the Grotrian diagram(グロトリアン図)による (p.404).
Worked: $3s\to2s$ has $\Delta l=0$, so it is forbidden (禁制). $3p\to2p$ and $3d\to2s$ are forbidden for the same reason.

4 Effective nuclear charge & Slater's rules (有効核電荷とスレーター則)

$Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=Z-\sigma$ ($\sigma$: screening constant, スクリーニング定数)
Prerequisite — orbitals per subshell & max electrons (needed to know how many electrons go in each Slater's-rule group)
subshell$l$# orbitals $(2l+1)$max electrons $2(2l+1)$
s0$1$$2$
p1$3$$6$
d2$5$$10$
f3$7$$14$
By definition (副殻の軌道の数と最大収容電子数)による — summing $2(2l+1)$ over $l=0,\dots,n-1$ gives the shell total, max electrons in shell $n$ = $2n^2$.
Q1 Is the other electron outside (farther out than) the electron of interest?
Contribution = $0$ (electrons farther out contribute nothing to the screening)
By Slater's rule(スレーター則)による.
Q2 Is it in the same group as the electron of interest? (Groups: 1s / 2s,2p / 3s,3p / 3d / 4s,4p / 4d / 4f / 5s,5p / 5d …)
Contribution = $0.35$ (except within the 1s group, where it is $0.30$)
By Slater's rule(スレーター則)による.
Q3 Is it inside (closer in than) the electron of interest? (inner electrons always contribute fully)
If that inner electron is in the (n−1) shell AND the electron of interest is in an ns or np group, contribution = $0.85$; for every other, even-more-inner electron (or whenever the electron of interest is nd/nf), contribution = $1.00$
By Slater's rule(スレーター則)による — this is the key difference from ns,np: nd and nf orbitals always receive full shielding (1.00) from inner electrons.
Worked example: $_{26}\mathrm{Fe}$: $1s^2\,2s^2 2p^6\,3s^2 3p^6\,3d^6\,4s^2$
$\sigma(1s)=0.30\times1=0.30\ \Rightarrow Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=25.70$
$\sigma(2s,2p)=0.35\times7+0.85\times2=4.15\ \Rightarrow Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=21.85$
$\sigma(3s,3p)=0.35\times7+0.85\times8+1.00\times2=11.25\ \Rightarrow Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=14.75$
$\sigma(3d)=0.35\times5+1.00\times18=19.75\ \Rightarrow Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=6.25$
$\sigma(4s)=0.35\times1+0.85\times14+1.00\times10=22.25\ \Rightarrow Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=$$3.75$ (the effective nuclear charge felt by the outermost electron)
[!] Trap: from a $3d$ electron's point of view, a $4s$ electron is farther out, so it contributes 0 — this is why $3d$ is screened less than $4s$ and therefore feels a larger $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (→ $3d$ ends up more stable than $4s$, which underlies the Cr/Cu exceptions).

5 Ground-state term of a multi-electron atom (Hund's rule) (多電子原子の基底状態のスペクトル項)

Closed subshells have $L=S=0$ and can be ignored; only the electrons in the open subshell matter.
Prerequisite — why energy depends on both $n$ and $l$ in multi-electron atoms (unlike hydrogen)
The building-up (Aufbau) principle(構成原理): fill the lowest-energy orbital first (Pauli's exclusion principle limits each orbital to 2 electrons); for equal $n$, fill smaller $l$ first (s, p, d, f order); for degenerate orbitals, spread electrons into different orbitals with parallel spins first (Hund's rule).
By the Aufbau principle(構成原理)により.
Why $s$ sits below $p$ in energy: the $2s$ orbital partially penetrates inside the $1s$ shell, so a $2s$ electron feels a larger effective nuclear charge than a $2p$ electron does — it's held more tightly and confined to a smaller region, lowering its energy.
Why $4s$ fills before $3d$: even though $n=4>3$, putting the next electron in $4s$ gives a lower total atomic energy than putting it in $3d$ — so K is $[\mathrm{Ar}]4s^1$ and Ca is $[\mathrm{Ar}]4s^2$, not $3d^1$/$3d^2$.
Cr and Cu exceptions (3d and 4s energies become very close across period 4)
Cr: expected $[\mathrm{Ar}]4s^23d^4$, actual ground state = $[\mathrm{Ar}]4s^13d^5$. Cu: expected $[\mathrm{Ar}]4s^23d^9$, actual ground state = $[\mathrm{Ar}]4s^13d^{10}$.
By the Aufbau principle(構成原理)による exception — a half-filled ($d^5$) or completely filled ($d^{10}$) $3d$ subshell, with every $3d$ electron spin-aligned, is more stable than the naive fill order predicts.
Q1 Have you found $L_{\max}, S_{\max}$? (fill electrons starting at $m_l=+l$ with $m_s=+1/2$; once every $m_l$ has one electron, continue filling from $m_l=+l$ again with $m_s=-1/2$)
$S_{\max}=\sum m_s$ (multiplicity $2S_{\max}+1$),   $L_{\max}=\sum m_l$ (for that filling)
By the Aufbau principle(構成原理)により and the coupling of angular momenta in multi-electron atoms(多電子原子の角運動量の合成) — term-symbol letters: $L=0,1,2,3,4\Rightarrow$$S,P,D,F,G$.
Q2 Is the open subshell filled to half or less, or more than half?
Half or less: $J=$$J_{\min}=|L-S|$; more than half: $J=$$J_{\max}=L+S$
By Hund's rule(フントの規則)により → final term symbol $^{2S+1}L_J$.
Example: Co (27): $[\mathrm{Ar}]4s^23d^7$ → open subshell is $3d^7$
Fill $\uparrow$ into $m_l=+2,+1,0,-1,-2$ (5 electrons, Hund's rule maximizes spin first), then the remaining 2 electrons go in as $\downarrow$ starting again at $m_l=+2,+1$.
$L_{\max}=2+1+0-1-2+2+1=$$3$ → F term.   $S_{\max}=\tfrac12\times5-\tfrac12\times2=$$\dfrac32$
$3d^7$ is more than half-filled (7>5), so $J=J_{\max}=L+S=3+\tfrac32=\tfrac92$
∴ ground-state term of Co = $^4F_{9/2}$
Contrast example: Ag (47): $[\mathrm{Kr}]5s^14d^{10}$ → the only open subshell is $5s^1$ (4d is closed)
Only one unpaired electron, in $5s$, so $L=0$, $S=1/2$ → term is $^2S_{1/2}$ (zero orbital-angular-momentum contribution; only $M_s=\pm1/2$ exists).
[!] Trap: for an excited-state term (e.g. Na's D lines, 3p→3s), you cannot use Hund's rule (which minimizes energy) — apply $j=l+s,\dots,|l-s|$ directly to the given configuration instead. Example: $[\mathrm{Ne}]3p^1$ gives $L=1,S=1/2\Rightarrow J=3/2,1/2$, so both $^2P_{3/2}$ and $^2P_{1/2}$ levels exist (the origin of the two D lines at 589.0/589.6 nm).

6 H₂⁺ and the valence-bond vs. molecular-orbital methods (共有結合と水素分子イオン, p.431-436, 447)

Q1 Are electrons "localized on one atom" (VB), or "delocalized over the whole molecule" (MO)?
Valence-bond method (VB): $\psi^{VB}(r_1,r_2)=c_1\chi_A(r_1)\chi_B(r_2)+c_2\chi_A(r_2)\chi_B(r_1)$ — electrons are localized on each atomic orbital (AO)
By the valence-bond method(原子価結合法)による (p.436).
Molecular-orbital method (MO): $\psi^{MO}(r_i)=c_{Ai}\chi_A(r_i)+c_{Bi}\chi_B(r_i)$, LCAO (原子軌道の一次結合)
By the molecular-orbital method(分子軌道法)による (p.436) → expanding $\psi^{MO}(r_1)\psi^{MO}(r_2)$ shows it contains the VB terms plus extra ionic terms $A^-B^+,A^+B^-$.
Q2 Asked to find the bonding/antibonding orbital energies of H₂⁺ by the variational method?
Full derivation, step by step (this is what problem 4-13 walks through) — start from $\psi=c_A\chi_A+c_B\chi_B$
(1) Define the three integrals using the Hamiltonian $\hat H$:
Coulomb integral: $H_{ii}=$$\displaystyle\int\chi_i^*\hat H\chi_i\,d\tau$ ($i=A,B$)
Resonance integral: $H_{AB}=$$\displaystyle\int\chi_A^*\hat H\chi_B\,d\tau=\int\chi_B^*\hat H\chi_A\,d\tau=H_{BA}$
Overlap integral: $S=$$\displaystyle\int\chi_A^*\chi_B\,d\tau=\int\chi_B^*\chi_A\,d\tau$

(2) Write $E$ as a ratio of integrals, then expand $\psi$ inside both: $$E=\frac{\int\psi^*\hat H\psi\,d\tau}{\int\psi^*\psi\,d\tau}=$$$\dfrac{c_A^2H_{AA}+2c_Ac_BH_{AB}+c_B^2H_{BB}}{c_A^2+c_B^2+2c_Ac_BS}$

(3) Apply the variational condition $\dfrac{\partial E}{\partial c_A}=0,\ \dfrac{\partial E}{\partial c_B}=0$ to the ratio above. Differentiating with respect to $c_A$ (product/quotient rule, then setting the numerator of the result to zero) gives: $$c_A(H_{AA}-E)+c_B(H_{AB}-ES)=0$$ and differentiating with respect to $c_B$ gives the partner equation: $$c_A(H_{BA}-ES)+c_B(H_{BB}-E)=0$$ Writing these two simultaneous equations as a matrix: $$\begin{pmatrix}H_{AA}-E & H_{AB}-ES\\ H_{BA}-ES & H_{BB}-E\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_A\\c_B\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}$$ A nonzero $(c_A,c_B)$ solution exists only if this matrix's determinant is zero — that determinant condition is exactly the secular determinant from Q2 below.

(4) Solve: expanding $(H_{AA}-E)(H_{BB}-E)-(H_{AB}-ES)^2=0$ into the quadratic $E^2(1-S^2)-E(H_{AA}+H_{BB}-2H_{AB}S)+(H_{AA}H_{BB}-H_{AB}^2)=0$ and solving for $E$ gives $E_1,E_2$ below.
Secular determinant(永年行列式): $(H_{AA}-E)(H_{BB}-E)-(H_{AB}-ES)^2=0$; for the homonuclear case $H_{AA}=H_{BB}=\alpha$, $H_{AB}=H_{BA}=\beta$ ($\alpha,\beta<0$)
$E_1=$$\dfrac{\alpha+\beta}{1+S}$ (bonding, lower),   $E_2=$$\dfrac{\alpha-\beta}{1-S}$ (antibonding, higher)
By the variational method for H₂⁺'s MO(H₂⁺のMO法による変分法)により (p.431) — the overlap integral between the same orbital is $S=1$, and between different atoms it is $0<S<1$.
Worked: the wavefunctions are $\psi_1=\dfrac{\chi_A+\chi_B}{\sqrt{2+2S}}$ (bonding, $\sigma_g$), $\psi_2=\dfrac{\chi_A-\chi_B}{\sqrt{2-2S}}$ (antibonding, $\sigma_u$).
Why H₂⁺ is stable: plot kinetic energy $T$, potential energy $V$, and total $E=T+V$ vs. nuclear separation $R$ — $E$ has a minimum at the equilibrium bond length $R_{eq}=2a_0$.
By the virial theorem(ビリアル定理)により, at that equilibrium point specifically: $V=-2T$.
Bond order = (number of bonding electrons − number of antibonding electrons)/2
By the bond-order formula(結合次数)より.
Worked: $\mathrm{He_2^+}$ (3 electrons, $\sigma_g^2\sigma_u^1$): bond order $0.5$. $\mathrm{He_2^{2+}}$ (2 electrons, $\sigma_g^2$): bond order $1$. So the bond energy is expected to follow $\mathrm{He_2^{2+}}>\mathrm{He_2^+}$.
[!] Trap: neutral $\mathrm{He_2}$ (4 electrons, $\sigma_g^2\sigma_u^2$) has bond order $0$, so it does not exist as a stable species — don't assume "more electrons always means more stable."

7 Molecular orbitals of homonuclear & heteronuclear diatomics (等核・異核二原子分子, p.440-451)

Q1 Asked about the electron configuration/magnetism of a period-2 homonuclear diatomic (Li₂〜F₂)?
MO energy order (Li₂〜N₂): $\sigma_{2s}<\sigma_{2s}^*<\pi_{2p}(\times2, \text{degenerate})<\sigma_{2p}<\pi_{2p}^*(\times2)<\sigma_{2p}^*$
For O₂ and F₂, the $\sigma_{2p}$ and $\pi_{2p}$ order is swapped: $\sigma_{2s}<\sigma_{2s}^*<\sigma_{2p}<\pi_{2p}(\times2)<\pi_{2p}^*(\times2)<\sigma_{2p}^*$
By homonuclear diatomic molecules(等核二原子分子)による (p.440-442). Bond order = (number of bonding electrons − number of antibonding electrons)/2.
Worked: $\mathrm{N_2}$: bond order 3, all electrons paired → diamagnetic (not paramagnetic). $\mathrm{O_2}$: Hund's rule puts 2 unpaired electrons in the degenerate $\pi_{2p}^*$ orbitals → paramagnetic — this can only be explained by the orbital picture, not by a Lewis structure.
Q2 Asked for the MO energies of a heteronuclear diatomic (e.g. HF, CO, NO)?
The secular determinant has the same form as for H₂⁺, but now $H_{AA}=\alpha_A\neq H_{BB}=\alpha_B$ (approximating $S=0$)
$E=$$\dfrac{\alpha_A+\alpha_B}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\alpha_A-\alpha_B}{2}\right)^2+\beta^2}$
By the MO energies of heteronuclear diatomics(異核二原子分子のMOエネルギー)より (p.451) — the bonding-orbital coefficient leans toward whichever atom's $\alpha$ is lower (more stable/more electronegative).
Worked: in HF's $3\sigma$ (bonding) orbital, the contribution from F's $2p_z$ is larger than from H's $1s$, so the bonding electron pair is skewed toward F (→ a polar covalent bond).
Coefficient relationship for the heteronuclear case (an extension of Q2, not always required, but shows how $\Psi_1,\Psi_2$'s coefficients relate)
From the same homogeneous system as Tree 6's derivation, plugging in $E_1,E_2$: $\dfrac{c_{B1}}{c_{A1}}=-\dfrac{c_{A2}}{c_{B2}}$, so if $c_{A1}=a,\ c_{B1}=b$ (with $0
By the MO energies of heteronuclear diatomics(異核二原子分子のMOエネルギー)より (p.451).
Worked: $\Psi_1=$$a\chi_A+b\chi_B$ (bonding),   $\Psi_2=$$b\chi_A-a\chi_B$ (antibonding) — notice the coefficients literally swap (with one sign flipped) between the two MOs.
Q3 Asked for the percent ionic character from electronegativity differences?
Hanny–Smith equation: ionic character (%) $=$ $16|\chi_A-\chi_B|+3.5|\chi_A-\chi_B|^2$
By the Hanny–Smith equation(ハニー・スミスの式)より, from charge distribution in chemical bonds(化学結合における電荷分布).
Worked: with $\chi_H=2.1,\chi_F=4.0,\chi_{Cl}=3.1,\chi_{Br}=2.8,\chi_I=2.5$: HF 43%, HCl 20%, HBr 13%, HI 7% — these agree well with the ionic character estimated independently from the apparent charge $q=p/d$ (from measured dipole moment and bond length).
[!] Trap: homonuclear molecules (H₂, N₂, etc.) have $g/u$ parity from their center of symmetry, but heteronuclear molecules have no center of symmetry, so $g,u$ labels are never used for them — orbitals are instead numbered in increasing energy order as $n\sigma, n\pi$ ($n$ = 1, 2, 3, …).

8 Hybrid orbitals and the VSEPR rule (混成軌道·VSEPR則, p.426-430)

Q1 Asked for the hybrid-orbital coefficients of a central atom such as carbon?
Rules: ① minimize electron-electron repulsion ② the $2s$ orbital is equally distributed among all hybrid orbitals ③ for each atomic orbital, the sum of squared coefficients across all hybrids is $1$
By the wavefunctions of hybrid orbitals(混成軌道の波動関数)による (p.430).
Worked, $sp^2$ (trigonal planar, 120°): $\psi_1=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}s+\dfrac{2}{\sqrt6}p_x$, $\psi_2=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}s-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt6}p_x+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}p_y$, $\psi_3=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}s-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt6}p_x-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}p_y$.
$sp^3$ (tetrahedral): $\psi_1=\tfrac12(s+p_x+p_y+p_z)$, and 3 more with sign patterns flipped.   $sp$ (linear): $\psi_{1,2}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(s\pm p_x)$.
Extensions using d orbitals (period 3+): 5-coordinate → $sp^3d$, 6-coordinate → $sp^3d^2$
By hybrid orbitals involving d orbitals(d軌道を含む混成軌道)による (p.427).
Worked: $\mathrm{PCl_5}$: $sp^3d$ (trigonal bipyramidal); $\mathrm{SF_6}$: $sp^3d^2$ (octahedral).
Q2 Asked to predict a molecule's shape / bond angle using VSEPR theory?
Procedure: ① draw the point-charge (Lewis) structure via the octet rule ② count the total $N$ of bonding pairs + lone pairs on the central atom ③ use $N$ to fix the basic arrangement ④ use the number of lone pairs $L$ to fix the actual shape
By the VSEPR rule(VSEPR則, 原子価殻電子対反発則)により.
Worked, $N,L$ table: $N=2,L{=}0$ linear ($\mathrm{CO_2}$); $N=3,L{=}0$ trigonal planar ($\mathrm{BF_3}$), $L{=}1$ bent ($\mathrm{NO_2^-}$); $N=4,L{=}0$ tetrahedral ($\mathrm{CH_4}$), $L{=}1$ trigonal pyramidal ($\mathrm{NH_3}$), $L{=}2$ bent ($\mathrm{H_2O}$); $N=5$: $L{=}0$ trigonal bipyramidal, $L{=}1$ seesaw, $L{=}2$ T-shaped, $L{=}3$ linear ($\mathrm{I_3^-}$); $N=6$: $L{=}0$ octahedral, $L{=}1$ square pyramidal, $L{=}2$ square planar ($\mathrm{XeF_4}$).
Small deviations in bond angle follow from this repulsion-strength order: lone-pair–lone-pair > lone-pair–bonding-pair > bonding-pair–bonding-pair
By electron-pair repulsion(電子対間の反発)による (p.426) — a bonding pair is also pulled toward the other atom, so it sits farther from the central atom (a lone pair is closer, hence causes stronger repulsion).
Worked: $\mathrm{NH_3}$ has only one lone pair (vs. two for $\mathrm{H_2O}$), so the repulsion is weaker and its bond angle sits closer to the ideal tetrahedral 109.5° than $\mathrm{H_2O}$'s 104.5°.
[!] Trap: in $\mathrm{SF_4}$ ($N=5,L=1$, seesaw), the lone pair occupies an equatorial position — not axial, a common wrong answer (axial would create more 90° repulsions).

9 Hückel approximation (Hückel approximation, p.452-460)

Q1 Setting up the integral rules?
Coulomb integral $H_{ii}=$$\alpha$ (same for every carbon), resonance integral $H_{ij}=$$\beta$ (bonded pair) or $0$ (non-bonded pair), overlap integral $S_{ij}=$$\delta_{ij}$ (1 if $i=j$, else 0)
By the Hückel approximation(ヒュッケル近似)による (p.452). ($\alpha<0,\ \beta<0$)
Q2 Asked to set up and solve the secular determinant for a chain or ring $\pi$-system?
Substitute $x=\dfrac{\alpha-E}{\beta}$, expand the determinant → a polynomial in $x$ → convert back with $E=\alpha-\beta x$
By the secular determinant(永年行列式)より.
Worked: ethylene (2C): $E_1=\alpha+\beta$ (bonding), $E_2=\alpha-\beta$ (antibonding).
allyl radical (3C chain): $x^3-2x=0\Rightarrow E=\alpha+\sqrt2\beta,\ \alpha,\ \alpha-\sqrt2\beta$.
cyclopropenyl (3C ring): $x^3-3x+2=0\Rightarrow E=\alpha+2\beta,\ \alpha-\beta$ (double root).
1,3-butadiene (4C chain): $x^4-3x^2+1=0\Rightarrow E=\alpha\pm1.62\beta,\ \alpha\pm0.62\beta$.
cyclobutadiene (4C ring): $E=\alpha+2\beta,\ \alpha,\alpha$ (double root)$,\ \alpha-2\beta$ — the total $\pi$-electron energy equals that of two separate ethylenes, so there is no resonance stabilization.
benzene (6C ring): $x^6-6x^4+9x^2-4=0\Rightarrow E=\alpha\pm2\beta,\ \alpha\pm\beta$ (each a double root).
Total $\pi$-electron energy $E_\pi=\sum_i n_iE_i$ — fill electrons into the levels found above (lowest first, Hund's rule for ties), then sum
3 electrons, chain vs. ring (allyl vs. cyclopropenyl): 2 electrons fill the lowest level, 1 electron goes into the next.
chain: $E_\pi=2(\alpha+\sqrt2\beta)+1(\alpha)=$$3\alpha+2\sqrt2\beta$
ring: $E_\pi=2(\alpha+2\beta)+1(\alpha-\beta)=$$3\alpha+3\beta$
since $\beta<0$, the ring's total is more negative (more stable) — the cyclic (aromatic-like) arrangement is favored even with an odd/incomplete electron count.

1,3-butadiene, 4 electrons (2 fill $E_1$, 2 fill $E_2$): $E_\pi=2(\alpha+1.62\beta)+2(\alpha+0.62\beta)=$$4\alpha+4.48\beta$ — only $0.48\beta$ more stable than two isolated ethylenes ($4\alpha+4\beta$), the modest "resonance stabilization" of a conjugated (but non-cyclic) chain.
Coefficients for the lowest MO $\psi_1$: solving the eigenvector equations and normalizing gives $c_1=c_4\approx$$0.372$, $c_2=c_3\approx$$0.602$, i.e. $\psi_1=0.372\chi_1+0.602\chi_2+0.602\chi_3+0.372\chi_4$.
Q3 Asked for $\pi$-electron density $q_k$, bond order $p_{kl}$, or HOMO/LUMO?
$q_k=\displaystyle\sum_{i}^{occ}n_ic_{ik}^2$,   $p_{kl}=\displaystyle\sum_i^{occ}n_ic_{ik}c_{il}$ ($n_i$: number of electrons in that MO)
By the $\pi$-electron density and bond-order formulas($\pi$電子密度と結合次数)より. HOMO = highest occupied MO, LUMO = lowest unoccupied MO.
Worked, benzene: by symmetry $q_k=1$ at every carbon, and every adjacent C–C $\pi$-bond order is $p=2/3$ — adding the $\sigma$ bond (1) gives a total bond order of $1.67$ (larger than the 1.5 suggested by a simple resonance picture). Butadiene: HOMO $=\psi_2(E=\alpha+0.62\beta)$, LUMO $=\psi_3(E=\alpha-0.62\beta)$; conjugation shifts its absorption to a longer wavelength (217 nm) than ethylene's (165 nm). Using $c_1=c_4=0.372,\ c_2=c_3=0.602$ (from $\psi_1$) and $\psi_2$'s coefficients, the bond orders come out unequal: $p_{12}=p_{34}\approx0.896$ (the two "double" bonds, each weaker than a full double bond) but $p_{23}\approx0.448$ (the central "single" bond has real double-bond character — consistent with its measured 1.48 Å length being shorter than a normal 1.54 Å C–C single bond).
[!] Trap: cyclobutadiene's $\psi_2,\psi_3$ are degenerate at $E=\alpha$, so by Hund's rule the two electrons go in singly, giving a triplet ground state — don't confuse this with butadiene (the chain form), which has no such degeneracy.

11 How to expand a secular determinant by hand (行列式の展開方法, 3×3・4×4行列式の求め方)

This is the general mechanical skill behind every Hückel/H₂⁺ problem — shown here on 1,3-butadiene's actual $4\times4$ matrix, worked exactly the way problem 5-10 does it.
Q1 Is your determinant $3\times3$?
Sarrus's rule(サラスの方法) — only valid for $3\times3$:
$\begin{vmatrix}a&b&c\\d&e&f\\g&h&i\end{vmatrix}=$$(aei+bfg+chd)-(ahf+bdi+ceg)$
By Sarrus's rule(サラスの方法)による — this shortcut does NOT work for $4\times4$ or larger; use cofactor expansion instead (Q2 below).
Q2 Is your determinant $4\times4$ or larger?
Cofactor (minor) expansion — pick one row or column, expand along it
Each cofactor = the smaller determinant left after deleting that element's row $i$ and column $j$, multiplied by $(-1)^{i+j}$
By cofactor expansion(余因子展開)による — expanding along the first row is the conventional choice(慣習).
Full worked example — 1,3-butadiene's secular determinant (4 carbons in a chain, $x=(\alpha-E)/\beta$)
Step 1 — write the $4\times4$ matrix (diagonal $=x$, bonded-neighbor off-diagonal $=1$, everything else $=0$; carbons numbered 1-2-3-4 in a chain, so only adjacent pairs are bonded): $$\begin{vmatrix}x&1&0&0\\1&x&1&0\\0&1&x&1\\0&0&1&x\end{vmatrix}=0$$ Step 2 — expand along the first row. Row 1 has entries $(x,\,1,\,0,\,0)$, so only the first two cofactors contribute (anything multiplied by a 0 entry drops out): $$=x\begin{vmatrix}x&1&0\\1&x&1\\0&1&x\end{vmatrix}\ (-1)^{1+1}\ -\ 1\begin{vmatrix}1&1&0\\0&x&1\\0&1&x\end{vmatrix}\ (-1)^{1+2}$$ Step 3 — evaluate each $3\times3$ minor with Sarrus's rule. First minor: $x(x^2-1)-1(x-0)+0=x^3-2x$. Second minor: $1(x^2-1)-1(0-0)+0=x^2-1$. Step 4 — combine (note the second term already carries a $(-1)^{1+2}=-1$, so subtracting it again flips back to $+$): $$x(x^3-2x)-1\cdot(x^2-1) = x^4-2x^2-x^2+1 = $$$x^4-3x^2+1=0$ Step 5 — factor. This biquadratic factors as $(x^2+x-1)(x^2-x-1)=0$ — verify by multiplying back out and confirming the $x^3$ and $x^1$ terms cancel. Each quadratic factor gives two roots via the quadratic formula: $$x=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt5}{2},\ \frac{1\pm\sqrt5}{2}$$ Step 6 — convert back to energies with $E=\alpha-\beta x$, ordering from most-negative $x$ (lowest $E$) to most-positive $x$ (highest $E$): $$E_1=\alpha+1.62\beta,\ \ E_2=\alpha+0.62\beta,\ \ E_3=\alpha-0.62\beta,\ \ E_4=\alpha-1.62\beta$$
Finding the coefficients (eigenvector) for one specific energy — worked for $E_1$ ($x=-\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$)
Step 1 — plug that $x$ back into the original matrix-vector equation: $$\begin{pmatrix}x&1&0&0\\1&x&1&0\\0&1&x&1\\0&0&1&x\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_1\\c_2\\c_3\\c_4\end{pmatrix}=0$$ Step 2 — each row gives one linear relation between the $c$'s. Row 1: $xc_1+c_2=0\Rightarrow c_2=-xc_1=\dfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}c_1$. Working through rows 2-4 the same way gives $c_3=\dfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}c_1$ and $c_4=c_1$ — so every coefficient is now expressed as a multiple of $c_1$.
Step 3 — normalize: $c_1^2+c_2^2+c_3^2+c_4^2=1$. Substituting the ratios above and solving gives $c_1=$$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{5+\sqrt5}}\approx0.372$, hence $c_2=c_3\approx0.602$.
Step 4 — assemble: $\psi_1=0.372\chi_1+0.602\chi_2+0.602\chi_3+0.372\chi_4$ (matches Tree 9's stated coefficients — this is where they come from).
For larger systems (benzene $6\times6$, naphthalene $10\times10$): hand-expanding is impractical — your course material itself says these are normally solved with software.
By convention(慣習)により — what you're expected to do by hand is (a) set up the matrix correctly from the bonding pattern, and (b) use molecular symmetry to predict qualitative features without solving: the number of nodes increases with energy, and node positions/coefficient signs for a symmetric molecule can often be read off directly (e.g. benzene's node pattern in Tree 9) rather than computed.
[!] Trap: the sign in front of each cofactor is $(-1)^{i+j}$, not always $+$ — for the element in row 1, column 2, that's $(-1)^{1+2}=-1$, so you subtract that cofactor's contribution (as done in Step 2 above) — a sign error here is the single most common mistake in this calculation.

10 DFT overview, spectroscopic selection rules, and relaxation pathways (DFT, 選択律, p.404-460, 566-580)

Q1 Asked about the conceptual difference between HF-SCF and DFT? lecture concept
HF-SCF: treats the wavefunction $\varphi(\mathbf r)$ as the unknown (deductive) and computes the interactions $V_C,V_E$ explicitly → iterates $\Psi,E$.
DFT (Kohn–Sham, 1965): works backward (inductive) from the electron density $\rho(\mathbf r)=\sum_i^N|\phi_i(\mathbf r)|^2$ to reconstruct the potential $V(\mathbf r)+V_{XC}(\mathbf r)$ and hence the Hamiltonian.
By the Hohenberg-Kohn theorem(ホーヘンベルク・コーンの定理, 1964)により and the Kohn-Sham equation(コーン-シャム方程式, 1965) (p.458) → the ground-state energy can be found purely by minimizing a functional of the electron density.
Worked: naphthalene's HOMO/LUMO electron densities and wavefunction signs are visualized via DFT, showing a nodal plane in the molecular plane. General-knowledge reconstruction: deriving the explicit exchange-correlation functional is outside exam scope
Q2 Asked for the 300 K Boltzmann factor of a given spectroscopy?
Boltzmann factor $=$ $e^{-h\nu/kT}$ (relative population of the excited state vs. the ground state)
As worked in Example 11-3, types and energies of spectroscopy(分光法の種類とエネルギー).
Worked: visible absorption (500 nm): $2.9\times10^{-42}$; IR absorption ($1000\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$): $8.4\times10^{-3}$; rotational ($20\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$): $0.91$; ESR (9 GHz): $0.9986$; NMR (300 MHz): $0.999952$ — because ESR and NMR have almost no population difference between ground and excited states, saturation occurs easily.
Q3 Asked whether an (atomic/electronic) transition is allowed or forbidden?
Transition dipole moment $M_{fi}=\displaystyle\int\psi_f^*\,\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\,\psi_i\,d\tau$ — if $M_{fi}\neq0$ the transition is allowed, if $M_{fi}=0$ it is forbidden
By the transition dipole moment(遷移双極子モーメント)による (p.404).
Individual selection rule (hydrogen-like atom): $\Delta l=$$\pm1$, $\Delta m_l=$$0,\pm1$
Multi-electron atom (LS coupling, the Laporte rule): $\Delta L=\pm1,\ \Delta S=0,\ \Delta J=0,\pm1$ (except $J{=}0\to J{=}0$, forbidden), $\Delta M_J=0,\pm1$
By the condition for an allowed transition(許容遷移の条件) — the Laporte rule(ラポルテの規則)により (p.407-408).
Worked: Na's D line (3p→3s) satisfies $\Delta L=1,\Delta S=0,\Delta J=\pm1,0$, so it is allowed. By contrast, an $ns\to1s$ transition between orbitals of the same symmetry has $\Delta l=0$ and is forbidden.
Homonuclear diatomic molecules: $\Delta\Lambda=0,\pm1,\ \Delta S=0,\ \Delta\Sigma=0,\ \Delta\Omega=0,\pm1$ plus the center-of-symmetry rule: $g\leftrightarrow g,\ u\leftrightarrow u$ is forbidden (the Laporte rule); only $\Sigma^-\leftrightarrow\Sigma^-,\ \Sigma^+\leftrightarrow\Sigma^+$ is allowed
By the selection rules for diatomic molecules(二原子分子の選択律)による (p.566).
Worked: in butadiene, HOMO ($\psi_2,B_g$) → LUMO ($\psi_3,A_u$) satisfies the symmetry condition and is allowed (labeled 3←2, 217 nm); by contrast $4\leftarrow1$ is forbidden.
Q4 Asked to identify fluorescence / phosphorescence / intersystem crossing (Jablonski diagram)?
Spin selection rule: $\Delta S=0$ — the spin multiplicity before and after the transition must be equal (the electron's spin direction does not flip on a ground-to-excited transition)
By the spin selection rule(スピン選択律)により (p.405).
Worked: $S_0\to S_1$ (absorption, allowed) and $S_1\to S_0$ (fluorescence $=$ fluorescence, allowed, $\Delta S=0$) are both fast. $S_1\to T_1$ (intersystem crossing $=$ intersystem crossing) and $T_1\to S_0$ (phosphorescence $=$ phosphorescence) both require a spin flip, so they are formally forbidden but become weakly allowed via spin-orbit coupling, making them comparatively slow.
Franck-Condon principle: the transition occurs vertically from the lowest vibrational level of the ground state, and it is strongest where the vibrational-wavefunction overlap between the two states is largest.
By the Franck-Condon principle(フランク-コンドンの原理)により (p.569).
Worked example — H₂ UPS (He discharge lamp, 21.2 eV): max./min. photoelectron kinetic energy 5.7 eV / 3.1 eV
Ionization energy: $I_p=h\nu-T$. The vertical transition (strongest, Franck–Condon maximum): $I_{p,\max}=21.2-3.1=$$18.1\ \mathrm{eV}$; the adiabatic (0–0) transition: $I_{p,\min}=21.2-5.7=$$15.5\ \mathrm{eV}$.
Dissociation energy of $\mathrm{H_2^+}$ $=I_{p,\max}-I_{p,\min}=18.1-15.5=$$2.6\ \mathrm{eV}$.
Dissociation energy of $\mathrm{H_2}$ $=I_{p,\max}-\mathrm{IE(H)}=18.1-13.6=$$4.5\ \mathrm{eV}$ (using the hydrogen-atom ionization energy of 13.6 eV).
As worked in Example 11.10, the H₂ UPS spectrum(H₂のUPSスペクトル) (p.577) — since $\mathrm{H_2}$ and $\mathrm{H_2^+}$ have different equilibrium bond lengths ($1.4a_0$ vs. $2.0a_0$), the Franck-Condon principle(フランク-コンドンの原理)により the $0\to2$ transition is the strongest.
Cell material for measuring UV absorption spectra: quartz (石英) (usable range 190〜2500 nm)
As worked in Example 11-8, UV-Vis spectroscopy(紫外可視分光法) — glass only works from 320 nm, and polystyrene/PMMA are visible-light only and cannot be used for UV.
[!] Trap: "the strongest absorption line" and "the $0$–$0$ transition (adiabatic ionization energy)" are different transitions — the electron always starts from the vibrational ground state ($v''=0$), but it lands wherever the Franck-Condon overlap is largest, which is not necessarily $v'=0$.

! Trap round-up (quick summary)

① $\hat l_x,\hat l_y,\hat l_z$ can never be measured simultaneously with each other, but $\hat l^2$ and any one of them can — always check whether the question is about "two components" or "the magnitude and one component."
② Orbital and spin magnetic moments have different $g$-factors ($g_L=1$ vs. $g_e\approx2$), so the same $m$-value gives different magnitudes.
③ In Slater's rules, $nd,nf$ electrons always receive full shielding (1.00) from inner electrons — don't apply the 0.85 rule meant for $ns,np$.
④ For a ground-state term: half-filled-or-less → use $J_{\min}$; more than half-filled → use $J_{\max}$. Exactly half-filled gives $L=0$, leaving only $J=S$.
⑤ Neutral $\mathrm{He_2}$ (bond order 0) does not exist, but $\mathrm{He_2^+}$ (0.5) exists weakly — "more electrons = more stable" is the wrong intuition here.
⑥ O₂'s paramagnetism can only be explained by MO theory (Hund's rule in the degenerate $\pi^*$ orbitals), not by a Lewis structure.
⑦ In $\mathrm{SF_4}$ (seesaw), the lone pair occupies the equatorial position — not axial.
⑧ Cyclobutadiene's degenerate HOMO ($\psi_2,\psi_3$, $E=\alpha$) gives a triplet ground state by Hund's rule — be sure to distinguish this from butadiene (the chain form), which is not degenerate.
⑨ $g\leftrightarrow g$ and $u\leftrightarrow u$ transitions are forbidden by the Laporte rule in homonuclear molecules — heteronuclear molecules don't even have $g/u$ labels to begin with.
⑩ Fluorescence ($S_1\to S_0$, $\Delta S=0$) is fast; phosphorescence ($T_1\to S_0$, spin flip) is formally forbidden but occurs slowly via spin-orbit coupling — don't confuse it with intersystem crossing (ISC, $S_1\to T_1$).
⑪ $4s$ fills before $3d$ going in, but once both are occupied, $3d$ is more tightly bound (higher $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$, per trap ③) — this is exactly why Cr/Cu "steal" an electron back from $4s$ into $3d$ to reach a half-/fully-filled subshell.
⑫ When expanding a $4\times4$ (or larger) determinant by cofactors, the sign $(-1)^{i+j}$ alternates per element — forgetting to flip it on the second, fourth, etc. term is the most common arithmetic slip (see Tree 11).

Top 3 things to review right before the exam

  1. Slater's rule grouping table (1s | 2s,2p | 3s,3p | 3d | 4s,4p | …) and the 0.35/0.85/1.00 pattern — remember that nd,nf electrons always get 1.00 shielding.
  2. The Hückel secular-determinant procedure: substitute $x=(\alpha-E)/\beta$ → expand the polynomial → convert back with $E=\alpha-\beta x$. Memorize the typical polynomial shapes for chains vs. rings.
  3. The three selection-rule tiers: individual ($\Delta l=\pm1$), LS-coupling/Laporte ($\Delta L=\pm1,\Delta S=0,\Delta J=0,\pm1$), and spin ($\Delta S=0$, for distinguishing fluorescence from phosphorescence) — first figure out which tier the question is asking about.
